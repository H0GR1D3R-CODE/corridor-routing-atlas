The problem

The fastest road is not the shortest road.

A city is a graph: junctions are nodes, road segments are edges. When someone in Yeshwanthpur calls an ambulance, the dispatcher needs two answers inside a second: which hospital, and which roads.

Both answers depend on this minute's traffic, not on the printed map. Outer Ring Road at 6:30 pm is a different road from the same stretch at 3 am. After a cloudburst, Silk Board may not be a road at all. A router that is merely correct on yesterday's weights sends the ambulance into the jam.

So the problem is a single-source shortest path on a graph whose edge weights are a function of time, queried over and over, with edges disappearing at random. The prototype below solves it, proves it solves it, and measures what it costs.

Honest scope. The – places, – hospitals, lakes and eleven highways sit at their real positions, converted from approximate latitude and longitude. The street mesh between them is generated, and the traffic is a model, not a live feed. The algorithms don't know the difference; the same code would run on an OpenStreetMap extract.
Input
Road graph G = (V, E): – junctions, – segments, – named places, – hospitals.
Edge cost w(e) = length ÷ speed × (1 + time-of-day + weather + events)  [minutes].
Origin s; destination d or the hospital set H; the scene (day, hour, sky, events); siren on/off; closed roads B ⊂ E.
Output
A path P minimising Σe∈P wt(e), with ETA, distance and mean speed. For dispatch: hospitals ranked by ETA.
Constraints
Weights ≥ 0 · answer in milliseconds · a wrong route is worse than a slow one, so the optimum must be provable.
Chosen algorithm
A* with an adaptive admissible heuristic for point-to-point trips; multi-target Dijkstra for “nearest hospital”. Dijkstra, Bidirectional Dijkstra, Bellman–Ford and Greedy best-first are implemented as rivals.

        The race

        Every plate answers the same question on the same traffic. The shared clock counts edge relaxations, the unit of real work, so the algorithm that thinks less finishes first. Watch where the ink spreads: Dijkstra floods in every direction; A* leans toward the goal; Bidirectional grows two lakes that meet; Bellman–Ford keeps re-reading the whole map; Greedy sprints, and sometimes sprints the wrong way.

          The benchmark lab

          Theory says Dijkstra and A* cost O((V+E) log V) and Bellman–Ford O(V·E). Here is what the stopwatch says. Eight cities from ~60 to ~8 000 junctions, 80 random trips each, evening-peak traffic, best of three timed passes. Straight lines on a log–log plot are power laws; their slope is the exponent.

            1. Edge costs are non-negative and geometric.

              A* needs a lower bound on the remaining cost. A road can never beat the straight line at the network's best speed, so the bound exists. Bellman–Ford's superpower (negative edges) is useless for travel time, and its O(V·E) bill is the price: see Fig. 5b.

            2. The weights change every minute and roads close.

              Speed-up schemes such as Contraction Hierarchies or ALT landmarks pre-compute shortcuts against fixed weights. A flooded road or a new rush hour invalidates them. A* has no preprocessing: a closure is just a number turning to ∞, and the next query already knows.

            3. The heuristic is adaptive, and provably safe.

              h(v) = (smallest minutes-per-km on any open road right now) × straight-line distance to the goal. It tightens when the network is fast and loosens when it jams, yet stays admissible and consistent. Test T6 checks both inequalities on every junction and road at three times of day, so A* still returns the optimum and never reopens a node.

            4. Dispatch is a different question, so it gets a different algorithm.

              “Nearest hospital” has no single goal for h to point at. One multi-target Dijkstra from the incident settles hospitals in order of ETA, giving a ranked list for the price of one search. The ticket compares that with running A* once per hospital.

            5. The honest limit.

              A*’s advantage is not constant. It expanded … of Dijkstra’s nodes at the quietest hour but … at the worst (Fig. 5c): when every road is slow, the straight-line bound is loose. And fewer expansions do not convert one-for-one into milliseconds, because every push now also evaluates a square root (compare Fig. 5a with 5b). Bidirectional search is a close rival and the better choice on a network with no coordinates (a metro map, say). For a million-node city with static weights, preprocessing-based methods win outright.

            Complexity, argued

            Time. Each node is settled once and each edge is relaxed at most twice (once per endpoint). A relaxation may push to the binary heap in O(log E) = O(log V), so the total is O((V+E) log V). With h ≡ 0, A* is Dijkstra, so its worst case is the same; with a consistent h the set of nodes it expands is a subset of Dijkstra’s. Bellman–Ford makes up to V−1 passes over all E edges: O(V·E).

            Space. Distance, parent and closed arrays are O(V); the CSR adjacency is O(V+E); the lazy-deletion heap holds at most one entry per successful relaxation, O(E). Total O(V+E) for every algorithm here.

            Measured. Fig. 5b fits the exponent from the data: Dijkstra’s edge work grew as V…, Bellman–Ford’s as V…. Dijkstra comes out almost exactly linear because a random trip floods a roughly constant fraction of the city; the log V of the heap is too small to show over this range. Bellman–Ford lands near V1.5, not the V² its bound allows: it needs about as many passes as the graph’s hop-diameter, and on a planar road network that grows like √V while E grows like V. The O(V·E) figure is the worst case, reached on long path-like graphs.

            Limits and next steps

            • Places are real; the street mesh between them is generated. Loading an OpenStreetMap extract only changes buildCity().
            • Speeds and the “siren clears 55% of congestion” factor are assumptions, not measurements.
            • No turn penalties, signals or one-way streets; the graph is undirected.
            • Weights are a snapshot at clock t; real systems stream live speeds.
            • Next: ALT landmarks (A* with a precomputed, still-admissible bound) and customizable contraction hierarchies that survive weight changes.
            07

            Colophon

            How it is built

            Plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript: no frameworks, no chart library, no build step, and no network use beyond the two typefaces. graph.js generates the city and the traffic model; algorithms.js holds all five searches plus the binary heap; tests.js is the ledger; lab.js the benchmarks and hand-drawn SVG charts; plate.js and race.js the renderers. Open DevTools and type CORRIDOR.g to poke at the live graph.

            Typefaces

            Fraunces for text, IBM Plex Mono for data. Offline, the page falls back to Georgia and Consolas.
